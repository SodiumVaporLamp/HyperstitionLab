Notes

How to read the spiral

Distance from the centre is drawn in log time. Every turn inward divides the time left by ten, so the turns hold millennia, then centuries, decades, years and months. A logarithmic spiral is the curve where scaling and rotating are the same transformation: zoom in and it turns, which is why travelling through it feels like a tunnel.

Six lanes run side by side, from the rim inward: Models, Capital, Reckoning, Learning, Language and Hyperstition. Milestones on the same date line up across the lanes. When two milestones in one lane fall almost on the same date, they are nudged apart along the lane so both can be tapped; the card always gives the true date.

The centre

By default the centre is the date in the title of Heinz von Foerster, Patricia Mora and Lawrence Amiot’s 1960 Science paper, “Doomsday: Friday, 13 November, A.D. 2026”. They fitted two thousand years of population estimates to a hyperbolic curve, the kind that reaches infinity in finite time. Population growth bent away from it in the 1960s. The date falls three weeks after this page was made.

“Now” puts the moment you opened the page at the centre. “2045” uses the date Ray Kurzweil gave for the Singularity in 2005; the inner turns then become empty future and the whole history crowds onto the outer turns.

Any list of milestones drawn in log time looks as if it accelerates into its centre. Recent events are easier to know about, and the axis gives them more room. Read the crowding near the centre as partly a property of the drawing.

What counts, and how much

The Models lane holds 81 releases: the major ones from OpenAI and Anthropic and the most notable from other labs, each drawn as a triangle pointing at the centre. Releases per year went from one or two before 2022 to more than twenty in 2025, and 2026 passed that by October. The log axis already gives recent time extra room, so the crowding of triangles toward the centre is the pace showing through it.

Frederick Jelinek’s line about firing linguists raised the question of whether linguists should count for less in this history. The page’s answer is to count the rule-writers for less. Markov, Shannon, Harris and Firth were doing linguistics by counting, and modern language models descend from that line. Hand-written grammars, expert systems and other attempts to type knowledge in are drawn hollow, as roads not taken. The Bitter Lesson view hides them, leaving roughly the history as the winners tell it.

Each milestone carries a weight. The 56 key milestones show up in “Key milestones only” and in the larger marks; the rest fill in the record. Before 1900 the selection follows two threads: the places where Land’s story touches the past (zero and place value, double-entry ledgers, the oceanic trading companies), and the machine line from Llull through Babbage and Lovelace to Boole.

Whose words

On each card, “From the texts” quotes Nick Land or the Ccru directly and says where. “This page” is the page’s own reading in the same spirit, and is never presented as Land’s. Where a link might be expected but isn’t in the texts checked, the card says so: Land doesn’t name the Dutch East India Company in the texts checked here, for instance, so that pairing is the page’s own.

Main texts: Land, “Machinic Desire” (1993), “Meltdown” (1994), “Qabbala 101” (2007) and Crypto-Current; the Ccru Glossary; Brian Rotman, Signifying Nothing (1987); Richard Sutton, “The Bitter Lesson” (2019); Peter Norvig on Chomsky. Every card lists its own sources.
