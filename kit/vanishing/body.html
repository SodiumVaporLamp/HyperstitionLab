      How to read the spiral

      The spiral is drawn in log time. Every turn inward divides the time left by ten, so the turns hold millennia, then centuries, decades, years and months, while on screen each turn is two and a half times smaller than the one outside it. A logarithmic spiral is the curve where scaling and rotating are the same transformation: zoom in and it turns, which is why travelling through it feels like a tunnel.

      The same curve is the course of a ship that holds one compass bearing: steer about eight degrees north of due west and, seen from above the North Pole in stereographic projection, your track is this spiral, closing in two and a half times with each turn. Mercator’s map of 1569 is in effect a logarithm of that view, which is why it draws such courses as straight lines; it would draw this spiral as a straight line of log time. Each drawing keeps some relations true and gives up others. Mercator keeps directions and swells areas toward the poles; the spiral keeps ratios of the time left and shrinks equal spans of years toward the rim.

      Six lanes run side by side, from the rim inward: Models, Capital, Reckoning, Learning, Language and Hyperstition. Milestones on the same date line up across the lanes. When two milestones in one lane fall almost on the same date, they are nudged apart along the lane so both can be tapped; the card always gives the true date. Some cards end with links, under “Elsewhere on the spiral”, to the milestones they connect with.

      The centre

      The centre is “Now”, the moment you opened the page, and the page opens on the most recent milestone. Travel out from there to go back in time.

      “2045” uses the date Ray Kurzweil gave for the Singularity in 2005; the inner turns then become empty future and the whole history crowds onto the outer turns.

      Any list of milestones drawn in log time looks as if it accelerates into its centre. Recent events are easier to know about, and the axis gives them more room. Read the crowding near the centre as partly a property of the drawing.

      What counts, and how much

      The Models lane holds 93 releases: the major ones from OpenAI and Anthropic, xAI’s main Grok releases, and the most notable from other labs, each drawn as a triangle pointing at the centre. Releases per year went from one or two before 2022 to more than twenty in 2025, and 2026 passed that by October. The log axis already gives recent time extra room, so the crowding of triangles toward the centre is the pace showing through it.

      Frederick Jelinek’s line about firing linguists raised the question of whether linguists should count for less in this history. The page’s answer is to count the rule-writers for less. Markov, Shannon, Harris and Firth were doing linguistics by counting, and modern language models descend from that line. Hand-written grammars, expert systems and other attempts to type knowledge in are drawn hollow, as roads not taken. The Bitter Lesson view hides them, leaving roughly the history as the winners tell it.

      Each milestone carries a weight. The 58 key milestones show up in “Key milestones only” and in the larger marks; the rest fill in the record. Before 1900 the selection follows three threads: the places where Land’s story touches the past (zero and place value, double-entry ledgers, the oceanic trading companies); the machine line from Llull through Babbage and Lovelace to Boole, with the Antikythera mechanism alone far out before it; and the machinery for copying, steering, producing and connecting: Gutenberg’s press, Mercator’s chart, Watt’s governor, Evans’s mill and the Atlantic cable. Two early archives of writing sit at the rim, Uruk’s ledgers and the Shang kings’ questions to the dead.

      The body of the machine

      Most milestones on the spiral are papers, releases and announcements. In the Capital lane, Stargate and the Three Mile Island deal look ahead: up to $500 billion to be invested over four years, and power for Microsoft from a reactor still to be restarted. The two series below are money already spent and electricity already used. They come from different countries and different calendars, so each has its own chart, and neither measures spending on AI.

      Cash additions to property and equipment, from the cash-flow statements in Microsoft’s annual reports. They cover all its new facilities, data centres and computer systems, whatever they are used for. Buildings and equipment taken on finance leases are left out: the company records those as lease assets, not as cash spent.

      Metered electricity used by every data centre the Central Statistics Office identifies, whatever it runs; AI is not counted separately. The share of all metered electricity is as the CSO publishes it, to a whole percent.

      Forecasts

      The 20 forecasts were chosen by a rule written before any were picked: dated claims by the pioneers already on the spiral, by the frontier labs and their heads since 2023, by the first and latest of the large surveys of AI researchers, in one public bet, and in Land. Misses stay in with the hits. Each card quotes the forecaster’s own words with a page or a timestamp, says how the outcome is decided and who judged it, and links an archived copy. Forecasts are counted apart from the milestones.

      Looking ahead is the tunnel reversed: today at the centre and the far future at the rim, each turn out ten times further ahead. Open forecasts live there. Centred on now, keep going in past today and the spiral turns round to look ahead; go in past today again to look back. A half-filled ring is a 50 percent date, the point by which the forecast gives even odds, not a deadline. Resolved forecasts stay in the history views, with a thread from the day each was said to the date it named.

      Whose words

      On each card, “From the texts” quotes Nick Land or the Ccru directly and says where. “This page” is the page’s own reading in the same spirit, and is never presented as Land’s. Where a link might be expected but isn’t in the texts checked, the card says so: Land doesn’t name the Dutch East India Company in the texts checked here, for instance, so that pairing is the page’s own.

      Main texts: Land, “Machinic Desire” (1993), “Meltdown” (1994), “Qabbala 101” (2006) and Crypto-Current; the Ccru Glossary; Brian Rotman, Signifying Nothing (1987); Richard Sutton, “The Bitter Lesson” (2019); Peter Norvig on Chomsky. Every card lists its own sources.

      How this page is made

      SVL built this page with Claude, Anthropic’s model, in October 2026, and models still do most of the work of keeping it current. Two jobs are kept apart. A scout, a Claude session that runs daily on a schedule, reads the last two days of news and proposes up to three milestones, each as a pull request on GitHub: the card as it would read, its sources, the case for it, and what was left out or uncertain. The scout cannot publish anything. SVL merges a proposal or closes it. A separate session, also Claude, then checks each card against its primary sources, proposes corrections as needed, and republishes the page. Other model families periodically review the entire corpus, most recently Gemini 4 Argon in October 2026.

      Cards about Anthropic are drafted and checked by Anthropic’s model, and they attribute Anthropic’s claims to Anthropic. The same rules hold for every card, whoever proposed it: news roundups are leads, not sources; claims only the claimant vouches for are attributed; and Land and the Ccru are quoted only from texts checked at the time.

      Every proposal, correction and decision since November 2026 is public, as a pull request on GitHub. A page about machines taking over thought is partly made by them, and the record shows where.
